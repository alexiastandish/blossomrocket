Custom employee apparel is clothing decorated with a company's approved logo — t-shirts, polos, quarter-zips, outerwear, and headwear — that employees wear at work, at events, and in public. For most organizations, it is the most visible branded merchandise they own, because people wear it every week.

It is also one of the hardest categories to manage. Apparel comes in sizes, colors, and fits; employees join throughout the year; and every location seems to have its own vendor. This guide explains how company apparel programs work and where on-demand production changes the math.

## Custom Apparel vs. Uniform Programs

Branded employee apparel and uniform programs overlap, but they are not the same thing.

**Branded employee apparel** is everyday logo apparel: a polo for client meetings, a jacket for the sales team, a t-shirt for an event. Its job is consistency and visibility, so the priorities are approved logos, approved colors, good fit, and easy reordering.

**Specialized uniform programs** cover workwear with functional or regulatory requirements — protective, flame-resistant, high-visibility, medical, or industrial apparel. These programs often involve compliance standards, garment specifications, and sometimes rental or laundering services.

The difference matters because the buying process is different. If your team needs branded apparel that employees wear as a consistent "uniform" look, a company apparel program is a strong fit. If your team needs regulated workwear, confirm product availability and requirements with the provider first — Blossom Rocket confirms those requirements before planning a program rather than assuming them.

## Why Bulk Apparel Orders Go Wrong

The traditional way to buy employee apparel is a bulk order: pick a style, guess the size mix, meet the vendor's minimum, and store the boxes. That model creates three predictable problems.

1. **Size guesses go stale.** The size breakdown that fit last spring's team rarely fits this fall's team, so the smalls run out while the extra-larges sit in a closet.
2. **New hires wait.** Someone who joins after the order ships has to wait for the next batch, or gets whatever size is left.
3. **Brand drift creeps in.** When a location runs out, it orders from a local shop, and the logo, color, or placement quietly changes.

Each problem has the same cause: the order happens before anyone knows who needs what.

## How On-Demand Employee Apparel Works

On-demand production reverses the order of operations. Each item is decorated after an employee orders it, so the program never has to predict sizes or quantities. SplashBrands describes its company apparel stores as having [no minimum order requirements and no inventory management costs](https://splashbrands.com/company-apparel-store-for-employees/), with orders typically shipping in 2–5 days.

A company apparel program built on a [Company Store](/solutions/company-stores) typically works in five steps:

1. **Approve the brand rules once.** Blossom Rocket and your team define the approved logos, logo variations, product colors, and apparel styles.
2. **Build the store.** SplashBrands sets up an online store that only offers those approved combinations, plus any order approvals, budgets, or user groups the program needs.
3. **Employees order what they need.** Each person picks their own size and quantity — one polo or a full set.
4. **Decorate in-house.** SplashBrands decorates each order in-house using methods such as embroidery, direct-to-garment printing, and screen printing.
5. **Ship to the person or location.** Orders ship directly to the employee, office, or event.

Because the brand rules are approved before anyone orders, every employee gets the same logo, placement, and color — whether they are the first order or the five-hundredth.

## Real-World Apparel Scenarios

Employee apparel programs usually start from one of a few situations:

- **New hire welcome apparel.** New employees order their apparel during onboarding, or receive it in a welcome kit through [On-Demand Kits](/solutions/on-demand-kits).
- **Multi-location teams.** Every branch orders from the same approved catalog, so a store in one city matches a store in another.
- **Partner and franchise apparel.** When apparel has to carry a partner's or location's logo alongside your own, [Co-Branding](/solutions/co-branding) pairs each approved logo with its own approved product colors.
- **Events and trade shows.** Teams order event apparel when they need it instead of drawing from a stockpile.
- **Recognition programs.** Gift certificates or store credits let employees choose their own apparel as a reward.

## Choosing Apparel Employees Will Actually Wear

Branded apparel only builds visibility if people wear it, so product choice matters as much as the logo. Favor versatile pieces — polos, quarter-zips, and lightweight jackets — over novelty items, and offer a small set of approved colors that match your brand identity.
