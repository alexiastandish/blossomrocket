Most comparisons of company store platforms line up feature lists side by side — logo uploads, product catalogs, mobile-friendly checkout. Almost every platform on the market can check those boxes. The comparison that actually matters is further upstream: how the product gets made, who's accountable for it, and what happens when your organization's ordering pattern doesn't match the demo.

Here's what to actually evaluate.

## On-Demand or Inventory? This Decision Drives Everything Else

Before comparing anything else, find out how the platform produces merchandise.

**Inventory-based platforms** require you (or the platform) to commit to a quantity up front — a batch of shirts printed and warehoused before anyone orders one. That usually means order minimums, a larger upfront cost, and a risk of unsold stock if demand doesn't match the forecast.

**On-demand platforms** produce each item after it's ordered. That typically means no minimums — one item or a thousand — and no inventory sitting in a warehouse waiting to be claimed.

Neither model is wrong, but they serve different needs. A single predictable bulk order (a conference giveaway, a uniform rollout) can make sense with inventory. A recurring, unpredictable need — new hires joining throughout the year, employees ordering whenever they want — is usually a worse fit for a model that requires committing to quantities in advance.

## Who's Actually Making the Product?

"On-demand" can mean two very different things depending on who's behind the curtain.

Some platforms are **brokers**: they take your order and pass it to a separate decorator or printer, who has their own queue, their own quality standards, and their own timeline. You're trusting a vendor you can't see.

Other platforms do **in-house production** — embroidery, direct-to-garment printing, screen printing, UV and dye-sublimation, engraving — under one roof. That generally means tighter control over lead time and quality, because there's no handoff to a third party in the middle of your order.

If a platform's answer to "who makes this" is vague, that's worth pressing on. Blossom Rocket's Company Stores, powered through SplashBrands, use in-house decoration methods specifically so fulfillment doesn't depend on an outside vendor's schedule — orders typically ship in 2 to 5 days.

## Approval Controls and Budgets, Not Just a Catalog

Anyone can put a logo on a storefront template. What separates a platform built for real organizational use is what happens *around* the ordering — specifically:

- **Order-approval workflows.** Can a manager or budget owner review and approve a purchase before it's produced? This is separate from brand approval (which should already be handled — every product in the catalog should already be pre-approved on logos and colors). This is about spend control.
- **Multiple price levels or budgets.** Can different departments, locations, or employee tiers see different pricing or spending limits inside the same store, instead of needing a separate store for each?
- **Gift certificates or store credit.** Useful for recognition programs, new-hire allowances, or letting recipients choose their own item from an approved list rather than receiving a single predetermined product.

These aren't exotic asks — they're standard procurement questions — but a surprising number of swag-store platforms are built for a single small team and don't scale to them.

## Does It Handle More Than One Logo?

If your organization only ever orders merchandise with its own single logo, skip this section. But if merchandise needs to carry more than one approved mark — a partner's logo, a division's logo, a location's logo, alongside your own — confirm the platform supports **multiple logo variations natively**. That means uploading more than one approved logo, pairing each one with its own approved product colors, and letting the system (not a human, order by order) keep the combinations correct.

Platforms that weren't built for this usually make you recreate the setup manually every time a new logo combination comes up, which quietly reintroduces the inconsistency a company store was supposed to eliminate.

## Reporting: Can You See What's Actually Happening?

A store with no reporting is a black box. At minimum, look for visibility into orders placed, spend by department or location, and participation over time. Without it, you're back to tracking a merchandise program the way most companies did before company stores existed — in spreadsheets, after the fact.

## A Short Checklist

Skip the feature-by-feature comparison and ask these instead:

1. **Minimums** — Is there an order minimum, or can I order one item?
2. **Inventory** — Do I have to buy and hold stock, or is it produced on demand?
3. **Production** — Is it made in-house, or brokered to a third party?
4. **Shipping** — What's the typical lead time, specifically?
5. **Approvals** — Can spend be reviewed and approved before an order is produced?
6. **Logos** — If needed, does it natively support more than one approved logo?
7. **Reporting** — Can I see spend and participation without asking someone to pull it manually?

A platform that answers all seven clearly is worth a longer look. One that can only answer the first two is a storefront, not a program.
